What this measures. A and B both play continuously and are never paused. One is audible (live), one is silent (armed) and parked ~1.5 s before loopStart. At loopEnd - xfade + correction the two volumes are ramped past each other in 10 setVolume steps, then the roles swap. correction = (liveTime - armedTime) - loopDuration, recomputed on every 20 ms poll from sub-poll-interpolated playheads — i.e. the fade trigger is slaved to where the armed player actually is, not to where its seek was told to land. armedErr is what survives that correction; watch whether it converges or wanders.

Seeks land late, not just wrong. A seeking player's playhead freezes until the seek resolves while the live player keeps moving, so aiming the armed player at live - loopDuration using live's position at issue time lands it short by exactly the settle time. The re-arm therefore aims at where live will be on landing, using the last measured settle, and the arm lead stretches to settle + 0.8 s when that exceeds 1.5 s. Without this a client with ~900 ms seek settle never locks on at all: every pass exceeds the correction clamp, holds, and misses.

Deliberate deviation from spec: the retired player is parked at loopStart-1.5 immediately after the swap and re-seeked ahead of the fade whenever the loop is longer than ~2 s. Without that second seek the armed player just runs on past loopStart for the whole pass and there is nothing to fade into. Both seeks are logged; the re-arm is the one whose landing error matters.

Failure modes watched: a player that never reaches state 1 raises the red banner (concurrent hardware-decoder cap, typical of Android WebViews). Ad playback is inferred from getDuration() departing from the baseline, plus any unexpected state transition, both logged. Autoplay policy is handled by muting both players, starting them from the single Start gesture, then unmuting once both report state 1. Caveat: setPlaybackQuality is deprecated and a no-op on many clients — if the checkbox changes nothing, that is why, and the decoder cap is not dodgeable through this API.

Why the crossfade cannot be short. The ramp is 10 setVolume calls, but each one is a postMessage across the iframe origin boundary that the player applies on its own event-loop turn, unacknowledged and untimestamped, and the setTimeout driving them is clamped and jittery in the low single-digit ms. At 60 ms that is 6 ms per step and the ramp is noise, so steps are floored at 20 ms each: steps = min(10, floor(xfade/20)), and 10 real steps needs xfade ≥ 200 ms. Default is now 250 ms. The step nom / step max columns are the scheduled vs. worst actual interval per pass — if step max runs well over nominal, the fade shape you heard is not the one in the code.

The floor under all of this: setVolume sets a scalar, it does not schedule a ramp. However long the crossfade, what you get is a staircase of discrete gain jumps, not a curve. That is the real difference from a Web Audio crossfade, and the likeliest source of an audible zipper artifact at the splice. Try equal-power vs. linear and longer durations to see how far it can be pushed down.

Chasing an audible pause at the switch. Three different things sound like "a brief pause"; the gap column separates them. It is the maximum time the newly-live player's currentTime stopped moving across the splice, next to that player's own rolling baseline staleness.
  • gap is near baseline → no decoder stall. What you heard is the fade curve. Two copies of the same audio a few ms apart are only partly coherent, so a linear ramp sums to roughly a 3 dB notch at the midpoint, plus comb filtering from the offset. At 60 ms that reads as a click; at 250 ms it reads as a dip, i.e. a pause. Switch curve to equal-power (now the default) and shorten the fade to test.
  • gap spikes, or shows BUF → a real dropout. Either the armed player was promoted while still buffering (the fade now refuses to start unless it is in state 1 and moving, and says so), or the park seekTo on the retiring player stalled its sibling's decoder.
  • park delay tests that last one directly. The retired player is parked this many ms after the switch instead of during it. Set it to 0 for the original behaviour; if the gap returns at 0 and disappears at 400, seek contention is your culprit.
Loop window jumping. loop » and « loop move the window and the playhead by one loop duration together: the window goes to [end, end+dur] or [start-dur, start], and playback moves +/- dur to match. Your position within the loop is preserved, so two bars into an eight-bar loop you land two bars into the next section rather than being thrown back to its start.

Both players advance at the same rate, so armed - live is frozen the instant the seek lands and never changes afterwards. No lead and no positional trigger are needed: the +/- dur offset is baked into the seek target and the fade runs as soon as the armed player is genuinely playing. The target aims at where live will be on landing, using the last measured settle, so latency is roughly settle + 250 ms — about 400 ms on a fast client, stretching on a slow one. Because the offset is fixed at seek time rather than triggered by position, settle misprediction shows up directly as jump error; it is logged per jump (offset X s vs wanted Y s) and scored in the armedErr column for jump rows.

Pressing again before a jump has fired accumulates rather than replacing, so a double press moves both the bounds and the playhead by two durations. A jump pressed during a crossfade is queued and fires when that fade completes, and a pending jump suppresses the deferred park seek that would otherwise clobber its target. Note that your offset into the loop advances by the jump latency, because the audio never stops — that is elapsed time, not error.

Playback rate applies live to both players via the dropdown or the −/+ steppers, not just at Start. YouTube silently ignores any rate outside getAvailablePlaybackRates() (logged per player at onReady), so each change is verified against what the players actually report and a mismatch is logged rather than assumed away. The playhead interpolator uses each player's own reported rate, and a rate mismatch between the two is called out explicitly — it makes them drift apart in media time, which shows up as a correction that never settles.
